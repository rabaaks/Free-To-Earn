01 / Why we picked this topic

Why we started
Free to Earn

Our project focuses on one policy question: should government require employers to pay a minimum hourly rate, or should each worker and employer be free to agree on a rate?

Free to Earn supports repealing statutory minimum-wage requirements at the federal, state, and local levels. Under that proposal, pay would be set through an agreement between the worker and employer rather than a government wage floor. The proposal does not guarantee that every offer would be higher—or that every job seeker would receive an offer.

We chose the issue because it directly affects entry-level work. A student applying for a first job may have limited experience, while an employer may be deciding whether a new position is affordable. Our argument is that allowing different pay agreements could give both sides more options; the important question is how that flexibility would affect workers with less bargaining power.

This is a proposal about minimum pay rates, not a proposal to repeal overtime, workplace-safety, antidiscrimination, or wage-payment laws.

The question we’re asking

What does a minimum-wage law
mean for a first job?

A minimum wage is a legal hourly-pay floor for covered workers—not a promise of a particular job, number of hours, or take-home income.

Supporters argue a wage floor prevents covered workers from being paid below the legal rate and can raise earnings for some low-paid workers. Critics argue that when the required cost of hiring rises, some employers may hire fewer people, reduce hours, or change prices. Employers can also absorb costs or improve productivity. The result depends on the size of the change, the local labor market, and the workers and businesses involved.

Our proposal is specific: repeal federal, state, and local statutory minimum-wage mandates, then let employers and workers agree on pay. That could allow some entry-level jobs to be offered at rates below today’s legal floors; it could also mean some workers have less protection against low offers. This is the central trade-off our project asks readers to consider.

02 / What we’ve been reading

What can the numbers
tell us—and what can’t they?

A

How some countries set wage floors

Eight OECD countries do not have a statutory national minimum wage: Austria, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Italy, Norway, Sweden, and Switzerland. That does not mean wages are unregulated: collective bargaining agreements set wage floors for large parts of the workforce in these countries.

All eight are classified as having very high human development in the UNDP’s 2025 report, based on 2023 data. This is an interesting comparison, but it does not show that one wage policy caused a country’s development. Each country has a different economy, history, and set of labor institutions.

HDI combines measures of health, education, and income. UNDP Human Development Report 2025, data for 2023.

B

How many hourly workers earn near the federal rate?

In 2024, about 82,000 hourly workers earned exactly the federal minimum wage, and another 760,000 earned below it.

The BLS explains that these counts do not tell us whether each worker was covered by federal, state, or local rules. Some workers may be covered by a higher state or local rate, and the figures do not describe all workers or explain why someone earned below the federal rate.

03 / Our point of view

What we’d change
and what we’d keep

01

Repeal minimum-wage mandates

Remove statutory wage floors set by federal, state, and local governments, rather than raising or lowering one particular rate.

02

Let workers and employers negotiate pay

Compensation would be agreed between the parties. Job offers should clearly state the pay rate, expected hours, duties, and any benefits so applicants can make an informed choice.

03

Keep other labor protections

Continue enforcing overtime rules, workplace-safety standards, antidiscrimination laws, and requirements to pay workers what they have earned.

04

Be honest about the trade-off

Without a wage floor, a worker could legally accept an offer below today’s minimum. Our case for flexibility is that it may make more agreements possible; whether that benefit outweighs the risk of lower pay is an important point of debate.

We know a policy change would not affect every worker or business in the same way. We support wage flexibility, but any serious discussion should also consider how people with less bargaining power are protected and how pay agreements are enforced.

04 / A project for students

How we’d bring this
conversation to campus

Jobs and pay are not abstract issues when you’re applying for your first job or balancing work with school.

We’d like to make space for students to ask questions, compare experiences, and hear different points of view. With campus permission, that could include an information table, a small discussion, or a survey about what students value in a first job.

We would share the sources behind our claims and make room for disagreement. Take a look at the flyer, or print a copy to share during a presentation.

Ask your representatives

Look up your state and federal representatives, read their positions, and ask how they think about wage policy and first jobs.

02

Compare the evidence

Listen to workers, students, and local business owners. Their experiences can add context that a chart cannot show.

Invite classmates to share questions and different views. Follow campus rules for events and information tables.
